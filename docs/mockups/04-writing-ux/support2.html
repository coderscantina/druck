5 Tolls and tariffs

The bridge at Golling

In 1612 the bridge at Golling still charged by the load, not by the carrier. A horse with two sacks paid four Kreuzer at the gate on the Salzach side, and two more if the salt was bound for Bavaria rather than for the archbishop's own markets Klein 1998, 41.

What a toll collector earned

His wage was fixed and small: twelve Gulden a year, a house by the gate, and four cords of firewood. What made the post worth having was the rounding. A load of three and a half Zentner was written as four, and the half Zentner's toll went into a second purse that no ledger names.

How much did a collector actually take?

Less than people think, and more than the archbishop knew. The wage was a pittance. But look at the strokes on the boards. They did not steal from the archbishop. They rounded, and the rounding always went their way.

Is that in the books?

Only as a pattern. Every load at Golling between 1608 and 1615 ends on a whole Zentner. Nature is not that tidy.

Back at Golling

The bridge is concrete now, and the gate is a bus stop. Nothing at the crossing says what was paid here, or by whom, or how the half Zentner was always written as a whole one.

Walk ten minutes upstream and the old road is still there, cut into the bank above the Salzach, wide enough for one horse and its two sacks.

The gate at first light

The gate is shut before first light. The collector counts by lantern, chalk in his right hand, and the horses steam in the cold while their carriers argue about the half Zentner. He writes four. He always writes four.

Smuggling

The boards themselves survive. One, from the winter of 1612, shows a column of strokes that stops halfway down, a week in which the bridge took no salt at all.

Nobody was charged. The collector that winter was the Pfleger's nephew, and the Pass Lueg road, two hours south, took twice its usual load.
